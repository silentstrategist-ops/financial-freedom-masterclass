Why A Normal Resume Doesn't Work Here

A standard resume lists responsibilities. It's built to answer one question: what were you assigned to do. Consulting doesn't sell responsibilities. It sells outcomes, specific, provable changes that happened because a specific person was involved. This is the entire task of this chapter, translating years of internal job language into the kind of language a paying client actually responds to.

The shift is precise. A resume says what you were responsible for. A consulting offer says what changed because of you, described with a clear action, a real scope, and a measurable result wherever a real number genuinely exists.

The Translation, Shown Side By Side

Compare these two descriptions of the exact same piece of work.

Internal version. "Managed the regional supply chain and a team of ten people."

Consulting version. "Led a ten person team through a full regional logistics overhaul, cutting delivery delays and reducing avoidable costs across the region."

Same work, completely different level of perceived value. The second version isn't exaggerated. It's the same true event, described in the language a client actually pays for, rather than the language a company used internally to file it away.

A Full Worked Example Across Three Different Careers

This translation works the same way no matter what field you're coming from. Here's how it applies across three different starting points.

A teacher, fifteen years in the classroom. Internal version: "Taught secondary school mathematics and managed classroom behaviour." Consulting version: "Designed and delivered a structured curriculum that measurably improved student performance across a class of thirty, while managing complex behavioural dynamics under real time pressure." This translates directly into tutoring, curriculum consulting, or corporate training, all built on the exact same underlying skill.

An accountant, twenty years in corporate finance. Internal version: "Prepared monthly financial reports and managed the bookkeeping process." Consulting version: "Built and maintained the full financial reporting system for a mid sized operation, identifying and correcting costly errors before they reached leadership." This translates into small business bookkeeping packages, financial cleanup consulting, or advisory work for growing companies still figuring out their own systems.

A salesperson, twelve years in business development. Internal version: "Responsible for client outreach and closing new accounts." Consulting version: "Built a repeatable outreach and closing process that consistently converted cold leads into long term accounts, in a market most competitors found difficult to break into." This translates into lead generation consulting, sales process design, or direct sales coaching for smaller businesses without an internal sales function.

Notice what's actually happening in each example. Nothing is invented. The underlying work is identical. What changes is the frame, moving from "what I was assigned to do" to "what actually changed because I was involved."

Building A Believable Narrative, Not An Inflated One

Every strong consulting narrative follows the same underlying shape, a clear before, a clear intervention you personally led, and a clear after. Not a job title sitting on its own. A short story with a real beginning, a real action you took, and a real, honest change that followed.

If you genuinely don't have a hard number for a result, describe the real scale honestly instead, team size, timeframe, how high the stakes actually were. A made up number that falls apart under one honest follow up question costs you far more credibility than a modest, accurate description ever will. Clients can usually tell the difference between confidence and exaggeration, and exaggeration is far more expensive once it's caught.

The Quiet Outreach Message

Once your narrative is built, the next step isn't a public announcement or a loud launch. It's a short, honest message sent to people who already know your work, framed as reconnecting and exploring a new direction, not as a hard pitch aimed at a stranger.

A message like this works best when it references something real, a past project you worked on together, a shared connection, a specific memory of working well together. It should mention, plainly and without apology, that you're exploring offering this kind of support more formally now, and ask if they'd be open to a short conversation, not a sale.

This mirrors something worth remembering clearly. Real trust travels through people who already know you, far more effectively than it travels through a stranger encountering a cold advert. A referred introduction is consistently more likely to turn into real paid work than a cold approach, across every industry this has been properly studied in.

The Mistake Worth Avoiding On Your First Draft

The most common mistake at this stage isn't understating your value. It's reaching for generic, borrowed language that doesn't actually sound like a real, lived experience. "Results driven professional with a passion for excellence" describes nobody in particular and convinces nobody in particular. A specific, honestly described moment, one real project, one real problem solved, always outperforms a generic claim, because specificity is what makes a story believable in the first place.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. Choose one real achievement from your own career and rewrite it using the action, scope, result shape shown above.

2. Read your new version back out loud. Does it sound like something a stranger would genuinely pay for, or does it still sound like a job description?

3. Looking at the three worked examples above, which one is closest to your own field? What does that translation actually look like for you, specifically?

4. List three specific, recurring problems in your industry that you already know how to solve, because you've solved them before, more than once.

5. Who are three people from your past working life you could reasonably reach out to first, simply to reconnect honestly, before ever mentioning a pitch?

6. Draft, in your own words, a short message to one of them that reconnects first, referencing something real you shared, without asking for anything yet.

7. What has actually been stopping you from sending a message like that until now?

Tracking Without
Being Creepy

The line isn't about whether you keep notes. It's about the intent behind them. Are you tracking to show up better, or tracking to extract?

#

Why Tracking Matters, Even For Something This Personal

Relationships and referrals are, by nature, a slow, human process. That's exactly why they're easy to lose track of without some kind of simple system. Without one, it's very easy to reach out to the same person twice in one month while forgetting someone else entirely for a year. A light, honest tracking system isn't about turning relationships into a spreadsheet of targets. It's about making sure you follow through on the things you genuinely meant to do, remembering a detail someone shared, checking back in after a reasonable amount of time, actually sending the resource you promised, rather than meaning to and quietly letting it slip.

As your network of contacts and past clients grows, this becomes even more important, not less. Ten relationships are easy to hold in your head. Fifty or a hundred are not, and relying purely on memory at that scale means real relationships will quietly fall through the cracks, not because you didn't care, but because no one can reliably hold that much detail in their head indefinitely.

What "Creepy" Actually Means Here, And How To Avoid It

Tracking becomes uncomfortable, for you and for the other person, the moment it stops being about genuinely caring how someone is doing and starts being purely about what you can extract from them. The line isn't really about whether you keep notes. It's about the intent behind the notes. Writing down that someone's daughter just started university so you can genuinely ask how she's settling in next time you speak is a form of real care. Writing it down purely as a manipulation tactic to build false rapport, to make someone feel closer to you than they actually are, is where it turns hollow, and most people can sense that difference eventually, even if they can't quite name it.

There's a useful way to think about this distinction. If your notes exist to help you show up better for the other person, remembering what matters to them, following through on what you said you'd do, that's genuine care organized into a system. If your notes exist purely to help you extract something from the other person, tracking exactly how close you are to being able to ask for a favor, that's the system tipping into something colder, and it tends to eventually show, even in small, hard to pin down ways.

The simplest rule: only track what you'd be completely comfortable telling the person you're tracking, if they ever asked. If the honest thought of them seeing your notes makes you wince, that's a clear signal something about the system, or the intent behind it, needs to change.

A Simple System That's Genuinely Enough

You don't need specialized software to do this well, especially early on. A basic spreadsheet, or even a simple notes document, with a handful of columns covers almost everything you actually need:

Name and how you know them. A quick reminder of the real context of the relationship, where you met, what you worked on together, how the connection first formed.

Last contact date. When you last actually spoke, not just when you thought about reaching out. This single column alone solves most of the "did I already reach out to this person recently" problem.

What you talked about or promised. Any detail worth remembering, a personal update, a resource you said you'd send, a topic they seemed interested in, a specific challenge they mentioned they were working through.

Next follow-up date. A rough, honest date to check back in, not so soon it feels pushy, not so far out that the relationship goes cold again.

That's genuinely enough for most people, especially in the early stages of building a coaching or advisory practice. The goal isn't a complex system with dozens of fields and tags. It's simply not letting real relationships slip through the cracks of a busy schedule. As the practice grows and the number of real relationships expands, you can always add more structure later, but starting simple is far better than never starting because the "proper" system felt too complicated to build.

Making The System Genuinely Useful, Not Just A Chore

A tracking system only helps if you actually look at it regularly, so it's worth building a small habit around it rather than letting it become one more document you set up once and never open again. A useful habit is a short, five or ten minute check once a week, scanning for anyone whose follow-up date has come up, and either reaching out or honestly rescheduling the date if the timing isn't right.

It also helps to keep the notes short and specific rather than long and vague. "Mentioned she's considering leaving her corporate job, seemed genuinely stressed about it" is far more useful, months later, than a vague "good conversation, catch up again sometime." The specific note gives you something real to reference and ask about next time. The vague one gives you nothing to work with at all.

Setting A Realistic Rhythm

A useful, simple rhythm for most relationships in your wider network is checking in every few months, more often for closer relationships, less often for more distant ones. This doesn't need to be a big production every time. A short, genuine message asking how something specific in their life is going, referencing what you noted down last time, is usually enough to keep the relationship genuinely warm without it ever feeling like an obligation on either side.

It's worth resisting the temptation to apply the exact same rhythm to every single contact. A close former colleague you speak to naturally might not need a scheduled follow-up at all. A more distant contact from years ago might genuinely benefit from a gentle nudge every few months, simply because without one, the relationship would otherwise drift into total silence.

The Honest Test For Whether Your System Is Working

A good, simple test: if the person you're tracking saw your notes about them, would they feel cared for, or would they feel managed? If the honest answer is managed, the system needs adjusting, not because it will get you caught, but because the entire point of this was never surveillance. It was simply making sure you show up, consistently and genuinely, for people who matter to your work and your life.

It's also worth periodically asking a second, related question: is this system actually changing how I treat people, for the better, or is it just producing data I never really act on? A tracking system that exists but never actually leads to a better, more thoughtful follow-up message isn't doing its job. The value isn't in the spreadsheet itself. It's entirely in what the spreadsheet helps you actually do differently.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. Do you currently have any system, formal or informal, for tracking who's in your network and when you last spoke? What does it actually look like right now?

2. Using the four categories above, name, contact, how you know them, last contact, what was discussed, next follow-up, sketch out what a simple tracking sheet for your own top ten contacts would look like.

3. Think honestly about the test above: if someone saw your notes about them, would they feel cared for or managed? Be honest with yourself.

4. What's a realistic check-in rhythm for you personally, given your actual schedule and energy, every month, every quarter, something else? Does that rhythm need to differ between your closer and more distant contacts?

5. Pick three people from your network right now and write down, honestly, when you last actually spoke to each of them.

6. Think of one specific, real detail about someone in your life that you'd genuinely want to remember to ask about next time you speak. Write it down now, before it slips.

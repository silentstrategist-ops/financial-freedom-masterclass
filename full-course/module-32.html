The One-Line Value Statement — Distilling Twenty Years Into One Sentence
Why One Line

You will be asked, constantly, in networking events, in LinkedIn messages, in introductions at conferences, in the first thirty seconds of a discovery call: "So, what do you do?"

If your answer is a job title—"I'm a Director of Operations," "I'm a senior engineer," "I work in financial services"—the conversation dies. The other person files you under a category and moves on. You become interchangeable with every other Director of Operations they've met. You are a label, not a solution.

If your answer is a one-line value statement that names a specific problem and a specific outcome, the conversation lives. Because now the other person is not thinking "oh, another operations person." They are thinking "wait—do I know someone with that exact problem?" Or "hmm, that's interesting, tell me more." Or "my colleague at [Company] is dealing with exactly that right now."

The one-line value statement is not a marketing slogan. It is a conversation tool. It is the thing you say that makes the right people lean in and the wrong people politely nod and move on. And that filtering is a feature, not a bug. You do not want everyone to be interested. You want the right people to be immediately, specifically interested.

The Formula

I help [Specific Target] achieve [Specific, Measurable Outcome] by [Your Unique Mechanism].

Let's break each component down:

[Specific Target]: Not "businesses." Not "companies." Not "professionals." A specific, identifiable type of buyer. "Series B SaaS companies between 50 and 200 employees." "Newly promoted VP-level engineers at mid-market tech firms." "B2B service agencies doing $2M to $8M in annual revenue." "Mid-market manufacturing companies with more than three active vendor contracts." The more specific the target, the more clearly the right buyer recognizes themselves in your statement.

[Specific, Measurable Outcome]: Not "improve operations." Not "grow their business." Not "achieve their goals." A concrete, observable, ideally quantifiable change. "Reduce inventory holding costs by 15-20% within six months." "Hit 90%+ sprint completion within two quarters without adding headcount." "Identify and capture hidden margin leaks adding 8-12% to bottom-line profitability within 90 days." "Reduce first-year attrition of newly promoted executives by 50%."

[Your Unique Mechanism]: Not "leveraging my experience." Not "using best practices." The specific method, framework, or approach you use. "By implementing a structured change-request protocol and facilitated alignment cadence." "By conducting a 90-day Margin Leak Audit using a proprietary project-profitability mapping process." "By building a delegation framework calibrated to the executive's specific technical-to-people-leadership transition."

Twelve Examples Across Different Domains

To make this concrete, here are twelve examples across different professional backgrounds:

Operations / Supply Chain: "I help mid-market manufacturing companies reduce inventory holding costs by 15-20% within six months by restructuring their vendor approval and reorder-point systems."

Engineering Leadership: "I help Series B SaaS companies hit 90%+ sprint completion within two quarters by implementing a structured change-request protocol between product and engineering, without adding headcount."

Financial Analysis: "I help B2B service agencies under $8M in revenue identify and capture hidden margin leaks in their project profitability, typically adding 8-12% to their bottom line within 90 days."

Executive Transition: "I help newly promoted VP-level engineers build working delegation frameworks in their first 90 days, reducing their first-year attrition risk by half."

Sales Operations: "I help B2B companies between 20 and 100 reps restructure their pipeline review process to cut average sales cycle length by 20-30% within one quarter."

HR / People Operations: "I help mid-size companies experiencing rapid growth reduce voluntary turnover in their first two years of scaling by building structured onboarding and manager-coaching systems."

Compliance / Regulatory: "I help fintech startups under 50 employees build their first compliance framework from scratch in 60 days, so they can close their Series A without regulatory risk scaring off investors."

Marketing / Brand: "I help B2B professional services firms over $10M in revenue reposition their thought leadership to generate 3-5 qualified inbound inquiries per month from decision-makers, without paid advertising."

IT / Systems: "I help mid-market companies migrating off legacy ERP systems avoid the typical 40% cost overrun by implementing a phased transition protocol with built-in rollback checkpoints."

Customer Success: "I help SaaS companies with $5M-$20M ARR reduce net revenue churn below 5% by restructuring their onboarding and 90-day value-realization process."

Legal / Contracts: "I help small-business owners with 5-20 employees understand and negotiate their commercial lease renewals, typically saving them $30,000-$80,000 over the life of the lease."

Training / Development: "I help mid-market companies build internal leadership pipelines so they stop losing their best individual contributors to competitors offering management titles, by creating a structured 'technical leadership track' within 120 days."

How to Write Yours (The Process)

Do not try to write your one-line statement from scratch in one sitting. That way lies paralysis and overthinking. Instead, use the CCRI database you built in Module 7.4.

Step 1: Look at your 12-15 CCRI records. Identify the three where the Impact was the largest and most measurable.

Step 2: For each of those three, identify the Target (who was the beneficiary—what type of company, team, or individual?) and the Mechanism (what specific thing did you do?).

Step 3: Draft three versions of the one-line statement, one for each CCRI record. Use the formula. Do not edit for elegance yet. Just get the structure right.

Step 4: Read all three out loud. Which one feels most true? Which one makes you feel a slight flutter of discomfort, as if you are claiming something that feels almost too specific, almost too bold? That discomfort is usually the signal that you are close to the truth. The safe, generic version will feel comfortable. The accurate, specific version will feel slightly exposing. Go with the exposing one.

Step 5: Test it. Say it to a friend, a spouse, a colleague. Not for their approval. For their comprehension. Do they immediately understand what you do, who you do it for, and what changes? If they say "oh, interesting, so you're like a consultant?"—you are too vague. If they say "wait, do you do that specifically for [X type of company]?"—you are specific enough.

The Permission to Be Specific

There is a fear that runs underneath the resistance to specificity. It goes like this: "If I say I help this specific type of company with this specific problem, then everyone else will think I can't help them. I'll be limiting my market. I'll be closing doors."

This fear is understandable. It is also backwards.

In practice, the more specific your positioning, the more referrals you receive, not fewer. Because when you say "I help Series B SaaS companies fix their engineering delivery process," every person who hears that and knows a Series B SaaS company with engineering delivery problems immediately thinks of you. You become top of mind for a specific, recurring situation. If instead you say "I help companies with operations," nobody thinks of you for anything, because the category is too broad to trigger a specific association.

Specificity does not shrink your market. It makes you legible within your market. And legibility is what generates inbound interest, referrals, and premium pricing.

You can always broaden later, once you have traction and reputation. But you cannot start broad. Starting broad means starting invisible.

The Filter

If your answer to "what do you do?" is a job title, the conversation dies. If your answer is a one-line value statement that names a specific problem and a specific outcome, the conversation lives. Specificity does not shrink your market. It makes you legible within it.

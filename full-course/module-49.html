Why "Life Coach" Is The Weakest Possible Starting Point

Calling yourself a general life coach, with no specific focus, feels safe because it doesn't rule anyone out. In practice, it does the opposite of what it feels like it's doing. A potential client scrolling past a generic offer has no clear reason to choose you over anyone else claiming the exact same broad title. Nothing about "life coach" tells them you understand their specific situation, so nothing about it earns their trust quickly.

A specific, well chosen lane does the opposite. It tells a potential client, within a few seconds, that you understand a problem close to theirs, well enough that they don't need to explain their whole situation from scratch before you'll understand it.

Where A Real Lane Actually Comes From

Your strongest possible coaching lane isn't invented from scratch. It's already sitting inside the Skills Audit you built earlier in this course, and inside your own lived experience of change, struggle, or a hard transition you've personally been through and come out the other side of.

A few honest questions worth sitting with properly.

What specific kind of change have you personally gone through, and come out the other side of, more than once? A career pivot. A period of financial stress you fully recovered from. A major life transition you navigated without falling apart.

What do people already, unprompted, come to you for advice about? This is often the clearest signal of all, because it's already happening whether you've noticed it or not.

What professional world do you understand from the inside, well enough to speak its exact language fluently, rather than needing it explained to you first?

A Full Worked Example Of Narrowing A Lane

Picture someone with fifteen years of experience as a mid level manager inside a large company, who eventually decided to leave and start their own small business.

A generic starting point: "business coach." Too broad, too generic, competing against an enormous number of other people using the exact same two words.

A narrower, more specific lane: "helping mid career corporate managers plan and execute a real transition into running their own business, without recklessly burning their financial safety net in the process." This lane pulls directly from lived experience, both the corporate world and the small business transition, and it speaks precisely to the exact fear and hesitation a client in that exact position is actually feeling, since it's a fear the coach genuinely lived through themselves.

Notice what changed. Nothing about the underlying skill changed. What changed is specificity, and specificity is what actually earns trust quickly with a stranger encountering your offer for the very first time.

Testing A Lane Before Committing Fully To It

A lane doesn't need to be chosen perfectly and permanently on the first attempt. It needs to be specific enough to test properly. A useful early test: could you write one clear sentence describing exactly who this is for, and exactly what changes for them by the end, without using a single vague or generic word anywhere in the sentence.

If the sentence still sounds like it could apply to almost anyone, the lane isn't narrow enough yet. If it sounds specific enough that a particular kind of person would read it and think "that's describing my exact situation," it's ready to test with real conversations.

Why Narrow Doesn't Mean Small

A common, understandable fear at this stage is that narrowing your focus this specifically will shrink your potential client base too much to be worth it. In practice, the opposite usually happens. A sharply specific offer converts a much higher percentage of the smaller, more relevant audience it actually reaches, compared to a generic offer that technically reaches everyone but genuinely resonates with almost no one. A narrow lane also naturally grows over time, through word of mouth and referrals, because people are far more likely to recommend a specific, clearly understood service to someone they know is in that exact situation, than a vague, catch all offer they can't easily describe to someone else.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. What specific kind of change have you personally gone through, and genuinely come out the other side of, more than once in your life?

2. What do people already, unprompted, come to you for advice about? Be specific, not general.

3. What professional world do you understand fluently from the inside, without needing anything explained to you first?

4. Using the worked example above, draft your own narrow lane in one clear sentence, naming exactly who it's for and exactly what changes for them.

5. Read that sentence back. Does it sound like it could describe almost anyone, or does it sound like it's describing one very specific kind of person's exact situation?

6. Who is one real person in your life right now who fits that exact description? If you can picture a specific, real person, your lane is likely specific enough to test.

7. What fear, if any, do you have about narrowing your focus this specifically? Is that fear based on real evidence, or an assumption you've never actually tested?

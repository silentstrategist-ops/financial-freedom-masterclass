Two Very Different Kinds Of Platforms

There are two genuinely different categories of platform worth understanding here, and confusing them leads to a lot of wasted time. The first is the general freelance marketplace, a platform like Upwork, where you build a public profile, bid or apply for projects, and work directly with a client over a defined engagement. The second is the expert-call network, platforms like GLG, AlphaSights, and Catalant, where the platform itself acts as a matchmaker, connecting your specific expertise to companies who need a short conversation, a longer project, or ongoing guidance, and you're paid by the platform for your time rather than negotiating directly with a stranger from scratch.

Both are real, legitimate ways to get paid for expertise. They just work quite differently, and they suit different kinds of people and different stages of a transition out of full-time employment.

General Freelance Marketplaces

Platforms like Upwork work on an open marketplace model. You create a profile describing your skills and experience, clients post projects or ongoing roles, and you apply or get invited to bid. Over time, as you complete projects and collect reviews, your profile builds a track record that makes it easier to win future work, often at higher rates.

The strength of this model is its openness. There's no gatekeeper deciding whether you're allowed in. Anyone can build a profile and start applying. The weakness is exactly the same thing. Because it's open to everyone, competition can be intense, particularly for broadly defined categories like "business consultant" or "marketing advisor," and standing out requires a genuinely specific, well-positioned profile rather than a generic one.

This is where the narrow lane work from earlier in this course pays off directly. A profile that says "helping mid-size manufacturing companies untangle inefficient supply chains" will consistently outperform a vague "experienced business consultant" profile, for exactly the same reason a narrow coaching lane outperforms a generic one. Specificity signals real expertise, and it lets the right client immediately recognize you as relevant to their exact problem.

Expert-Call Networks: A Different Kind Of Platform Entirely

Expert-call networks operate on a fundamentally different model. Firms like GLG (Gerson Lehrman Group) and AlphaSights maintain large networks of professionals across virtually every industry, and when a client, often an investment firm, consulting firm, or large corporation doing research, needs a short conversation with someone who has genuine, specific, real-world experience in a particular area, the network finds a match and arranges a paid call, typically billed by the hour, often at a strong hourly rate given how targeted the expertise being sought usually is.

Getting onto these networks generally involves applying with a detailed professional background, and being accepted is based on having genuine, demonstrable experience in an area companies are actively curious about. This might be deep experience in a specific industry, a specific role, a specific geography, or a specific kind of operational challenge. Once accepted, availability for calls tends to ebb and flow based on what topics are currently in demand, which is often tied to what industries or trends investors and companies are actively trying to understand.

Catalant works somewhat differently again, positioning itself more as a marketplace for independent consultants and boutique consulting firms to be matched with companies needing project-based strategic or operational work, often larger and longer in scope than a single expert call, closer to a defined consulting engagement than a single conversation.

Why These Networks Suit Experienced Professionals So Well

It's worth being direct about something here: these expert networks are, in many ways, purpose-built for exactly the kind of person this course is written for. They are not primarily looking for the newest, flashiest ideas. They're looking for people who have genuinely done the specific thing being asked about, spent real years inside a specific industry or role, and can speak to it with the kind of grounded, textured detail that only comes from having actually lived it. A twenty-five year old with a business degree and no real operating experience is simply not what these platforms are built around. Someone with fifteen or twenty years in a specific industry very much is.

How To Actually Position Yourself Well

On any of these platforms, the same underlying principle applies: specificity wins, generality loses. A profile or application that lists a broad, vague set of skills competes against everyone else with a similarly broad, vague set of skills. A profile that clearly states a specific industry, a specific function, and specific kinds of problems you've actually solved gives whoever is reviewing it, whether an algorithm, a recruiter, or a client scanning a list, an immediate, clear reason to pick you over someone else.

It also helps enormously to have concrete numbers and outcomes ready to reference, not vague claims like "improved efficiency," but something closer to "restructured a regional distribution process that cut delivery delays by a meaningful, specific margin." Specific, real outcomes are what actually build credibility on a page a stranger is scanning in a matter of seconds.

Setting Realistic Expectations

It's worth being honest that these platforms are not usually where a full income is built from day one. They tend to work best as one part of a broader mix, income while a coaching or advisory practice is being built up through relationships and referrals, a way to stay sharp and paid while other, more personally built channels grow, or a genuinely valuable ongoing income stream once you have several of them running in parallel. Very few people build a full income from a single freelance or expert-call platform alone, and it's healthier to treat this chapter's options as complementary tools on the modern menu rather than the entire meal.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. Looking honestly at your career, which specific industry, function, or kind of problem do you have deep, real, lived experience in, specific enough that an expert network would be genuinely interested in matching you to it?

2. Draft two or three sentences describing your specific expertise the way you'd want it to appear on a freelance or expert-network profile. Read it back: does it sound specific, or does it sound generic?

3. Think of one concrete, measurable outcome from your career, a number, a specific result, a specific improvement, that you could point to as real evidence of your expertise.

4. Which of the platforms described above, an open freelance marketplace or an expert-call network, feels like a better initial fit for your specific background and comfort level, and why?

5. How would you realistically want these platforms to fit into your overall income mix, a primary source, a supplement, or simply a way to stay sharp and paid while other things build?

Why Specificity Wins on Both

Whether it's an open marketplace or a curated expert network, the same principle applies: a profile that names a specific industry, a specific function, and specific kinds of problems solved will consistently outperform a vague "business consultant" positioning.

Open Freelance Markets
e.g. Upwork, Toptal
  • No gatekeeper — you can start immediately
  • Track record builds over time through reviews
  • Open competition — specificity is essential to stand out
  • Clients found, not matched — you need to apply actively
Expert-Call Networks
e.g. GLG, AlphaSights, Catalant
  • Platform matches you — no cold applications to clients
  • Strong hourly rates for targeted, deep expertise
  • Application-based — prior industry depth required
  • Call volume varies by market demand for your topic
Profile Positioning — Before vs. After
"Experienced business consultant with 20+ years in various industries."
"I help mid-size manufacturing companies restructure regional distribution, reducing delivery delays and freeing operational capital."

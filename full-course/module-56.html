Why The First Message Matters So Much

Reaching back out to someone you haven't spoken to in a while, especially with a new venture in mind, can feel awkward. There's a real fear of coming across as someone who only resurfaces when they want something, the kind of message that makes the other person's stomach sink a little when they see who it's from, because experience has taught them it's probably a pitch. That fear is worth taking seriously, because the first message really does set the tone for everything after it. Get it right, and it feels like a genuine reconnection. Get it wrong, and it feels exactly like the transactional pitch people instinctively brace against.

The good news is that a first message doesn't need to be clever or elaborate to work well. It needs to be short, warm, genuinely low-pressure, and specific to the actual person you're writing to. Most people overthink this step far more than it deserves, turning what should be a two-minute message into something they draft and redraft for a week before never sending it at all.

The Shape Of A Message That Actually Works

A strong first message to an old contact generally does three simple things, in this order. It reconnects personally, referencing something real and specific about your actual history with them, not a generic greeting. It shares, briefly, what you're up to now, without over-explaining or over-selling it. And it closes with a genuinely low-pressure invitation, a short call or coffee, framed around catching up rather than pitching anything.

Notice what's missing from that list: an ask. The first message earns the reconnection. The ask, if there is one, comes later, in the actual conversation, once real warmth has been re-established. Trying to combine reconnection and a pitch into a single message almost always weakens both. The reconnection feels fake because there's clearly an angle attached, and the pitch feels rushed because it's squeezed into a message that was supposed to be about catching up.

A Simple, Adaptable Template

Here's a starting template you can adjust to fit your own voice and your actual relationship with the person:

"Hey [name], it's been a while since [specific shared memory or context, working together on X, that conference in Y, etc]. I've been thinking about you lately and wanted to reconnect. I've recently moved into [brief, plain description of what you're doing now], and I'd genuinely love to hear how things have been going on your end too. Would you have 15-20 minutes sometime in the next couple weeks for a quick call, no agenda, just good to catch up?"

A few things make this version work. It's specific to your real shared history, which immediately signals this isn't a mass message copy-pasted to fifty people. It states plainly what you're doing now without oversell or jargon, no impressive-sounding buzzwords, no exaggerated claims, just a simple, honest description a normal person could actually understand. And it explicitly names "no agenda," which lowers the other person's guard and makes it genuinely easy to say yes, because it removes the unspoken question of "what does this person actually want from me" before it even has a chance to form.

Why Specificity Does So Much Of The Work

The single biggest difference between a message that gets ignored and one that gets a warm reply is usually specificity. A generic "hey, long time no talk, how've you been" could have been sent to literally anyone, and it reads that way. A message that references a real, specific detail, the actual project you worked on together, the actual place you met, a specific inside joke or shared memory, immediately signals that this message was written for this particular person, and no one else.

This specificity does two things at once. It proves you actually remember the relationship, rather than pulling a name off an old contact list, and it makes the message genuinely easier and more pleasant to reply to, because it gives the other person something concrete to respond to rather than a generic pleasantry.

What Happens On The Actual Call

The call itself should mostly be exactly what you promised: a real catch-up. Ask genuine questions about their life and work. Actually listen, rather than waiting for a gap to steer the conversation back toward yourself. If it naturally comes up, briefly and honestly share more about what you're building. If there's a clear, real opportunity for them to help, an introduction, some advice they could offer, a referral, it's completely fine to raise it plainly toward the end, once real reconnection has happened. What matters is that it comes after the warmth, not instead of it.

It's worth being honest with yourself here too: not every reconnection call needs to lead anywhere immediately. Some will simply be a genuine, pleasant catch-up with no further action, and that's a perfectly good outcome on its own. The relationship is the actual asset. Any specific opportunity that comes from it is a bonus, not the entire point.

Adjusting The Message For Different Relationships

The exact wording should shift depending on how close the relationship actually is. For someone you worked closely with for years, someone you'd genuinely call a friend as well as a colleague, you can be far more casual and personal, even a little playful. For someone you knew only briefly or a long time ago, a manager from early in your career, a client you only worked with for a single project, a slightly more formal, respectful tone usually lands better, since too much familiarity too soon can feel presumptuous.

The core shape, reconnect, briefly share, low-pressure invite, stays the same either way. What changes is the tone, the length, and how much personal warmth you can reasonably assume is already there.

A Word On Follow-Up

Not everyone will reply right away, and that's normal. People are busy, and a message like this, however well written, is easy to see, mean to respond to, and then lose in the shuffle of a busy week. A single, brief, friendly follow-up a week or two later, something as simple as "just following up in case this got buried, no pressure either way," is completely appropriate and rarely comes across as pushy, provided it stays genuinely light.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. Pick one real person from your network and fill in the template above with actual, specific details from your real history with them.

2. Read your filled-in message back. Does it sound genuinely like you, or does it sound like a template? Adjust it until it sounds like your own voice.

3. What's the actual worst realistic outcome if you send this message? Is that outcome something you could genuinely live with?

4. Think of a "no agenda" message you've received from someone else in the past. How did it make you feel, compared to a message that clearly had an ask baked into it?

5. Pick three people from your list and commit to sending this message to one of them this week. Which one, and by when?

6. For each of those three people, is the relationship closer to a casual friendship or a more formal professional connection? How should that change the tone of your message to each?

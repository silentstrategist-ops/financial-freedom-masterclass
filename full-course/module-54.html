Give Before You Ask, Every Time

There's a simple rule that quietly separates people who build strong networks from people who burn through their contacts fast: give something useful before you ask for anything. Not as a manipulation tactic, but because it's genuinely how real relationships work, and because it's the only version of networking that actually feels good on both ends, for you and for the person on the other side of it.

Most people's instinct, especially when money is tight or a new practice needs clients fast, is to reach out and ask right away. Can you refer me clients. Can you introduce me to your boss. Can you buy this. That instinct is understandable, the pressure is real, but it usually backfires, because it puts the other person in the position of doing you a favor before you've done anything for them. People help those who've already helped them far more readily than they help someone who's shown up only to ask, and this is true even when the ask itself is small and reasonable.

Why The Order Matters So Much

It's tempting to think the order doesn't really matter, that as long as value eventually gets exchanged both ways, it shouldn't matter who goes first. In practice, the order changes everything about how the relationship feels. Asking first frames the entire interaction as transactional from the very start, even if that's not your intention. It puts the other person on the back foot, weighing whether they owe you anything, whether they know you well enough, whether this is worth their time.

Giving first flips that entirely. It removes any pressure from the other person, and it lets them form their own honest opinion of you based on something real you've actually done, rather than based on a pitch about what you might be able to do. It also, quietly, tells them something important about your character: that you're someone who thinks about other people's problems, not just your own.

What You Actually Have To Offer, Even Before You Have Clients

A common worry at this stage is thinking you have nothing worth giving yet, no products, no case studies, no big offer, no polished business. This misses what people actually value most in a relationship. Before you have any of that, you likely already have some combination of the following, and any one of them is worth more than most people realize.

A useful answer. Someone in your network is stuck on a problem you happen to understand well from your career, whatever that career was. Taking fifteen minutes to genuinely help them think it through costs you almost nothing and is remembered for a long time. This is often the single easiest form of value to give, because it draws directly on knowledge you already have sitting in your head from years of doing real work.

A helpful introduction. You know two people who should know each other, and neither of them has thought to connect you two. Making that introduction, with nothing in it for you, is one of the highest value, lowest cost things you can do. It costs you a single short message, and it can genuinely change the trajectory of someone else's project or career.

A useful resource. An article, a tool, a template, a book that solved a problem for you, shared at the right moment with the right person. This works especially well when it's specific, sent because it directly relates to something the person mentioned, rather than a generic "thought you might like this" sent to a long list of contacts at once.

A genuine endorsement. Publicly or privately saying something true and specific and positive about someone's work, when it's actually deserved. A short, honest, specific comment or recommendation costs you almost nothing to give and can genuinely help someone's own reputation or business.

None of these require you to have already "made it." They require you to be paying attention to other people's actual problems, and to be willing to spend a small amount of your own time and effort on someone else's behalf without immediately expecting something back.

The Difference Between Real Value And A Disguised Pitch

There's a version of "giving value first" that's really just a pitch wearing a disguise, and people can usually tell the difference within a sentence or two. Sending someone an article that happens to end with "and this is exactly why my new coaching program can help" isn't giving value first. It's a sales pitch with an extra step attached to it, and it tends to erode trust faster than a direct, honest ask would have.

Real value stands entirely on its own. It should be just as useful and just as complete if you never mention your own work at all. If you find yourself unable to imagine giving something without immediately connecting it back to your own offer, that's usually a sign it isn't genuine value-first networking yet.

Why This Works, Not Just As A Nice Idea But As A Real Pattern

There's a well documented pattern in how people respond when someone does something genuinely useful for them without being asked: they feel a real, natural pull to do something useful back. This isn't a trick you're playing on people. It's simply how trust and goodwill build between two humans over time, the same way it works in a friendship, just applied deliberately to your professional relationships.

The key word is genuine. If the "value" you're offering is thin, self-serving, or clearly just a setup for an ask, people notice, and it does the opposite of building trust. The value has to actually be valuable to the other person, on its own terms, whether or not anything ever comes back to you. In fact, the moments where you give real value and genuinely expect nothing in return are often the moments that build the deepest trust, precisely because the other person can tell there was no hidden angle.

A Simple Way To Practice This Deliberately

Pick five people from your network. For each one, ask yourself honestly: what's one small, real thing I could do for this specific person this month, that costs me relatively little and would genuinely help them. It might be different for every single person. That's the point. Real value-first networking isn't a mass email or a copy-pasted message sent to fifty contacts at once. It's specific, small, and personal, repeated consistently over time, one real person at a time.

It also helps to think of this as an ongoing habit rather than a one-time campaign. Value-first networking isn't something you do for a month before launching your coaching practice and then quietly stop. The people who build the strongest networks over a lifetime tend to be the ones who keep doing this quietly, in the background, whether or not they currently need anything from anyone.

Reflect And Apply

Answer these fully in your journal.

1. Of the four types of value described above, an answer, an introduction, a resource, an endorsement, which comes most naturally to you to give?

2. Think of someone in your network with a problem you could genuinely help with right now. What is that problem, and what would helping actually look like, concretely?

3. Are there two people in your network who don't know each other but genuinely should? What would introducing them actually sound like, in your own words?

4. Recall a time someone gave you real, unasked-for value. How did it change the way you felt about them afterward? Did it change how you behaved toward them later?

5. Be honest with yourself: has your instinct in networking leaned more toward asking first, or giving first? What's one specific thing you could give this week, before asking for anything?

6. Think of a time you received something that felt like a "disguised pitch" rather than real value. What specifically made it feel that way?

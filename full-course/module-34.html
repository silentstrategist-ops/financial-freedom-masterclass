The Psychological Barrier — Why This Audit Feels Like Lying (And Why It Isn't)

The Imposter Response

As you work through this chapter, there is a very high probability that at some point—probably during the CCRI writing, probably when you are drafting your one-line statement—you will feel a specific, uncomfortable sensation. A tightness in the chest. A voice that says, "Who are you to claim this? Other people have done more. You're exaggerating. You got lucky. The team did it, not you. You're just doing what anyone in your role would do."

This is the imposter response. And it is worth understanding precisely what it is, so you can work with it rather than being stopped by it.

Research on imposter phenomenon (the term preferred by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, who first described it in 1978) consistently finds that it is not correlated with actual incompetence. It is most prevalent among high achievers, particularly those who have succeeded in environments where they were underrepresented or where the criteria for success were ambiguous. The more competent you are, the more aware you are of the boundaries of your knowledge, and the more you assume everyone else is operating at the same level of awareness. The imposter feeling is not evidence that you are unqualified. It is evidence that you are aware of complexity in a way that less experienced people are not.

The specific form it takes during this audit is the feeling that naming your achievements in specific, self-attributed terms is a form of taking credit that isn't yours. "The team did it." "My manager set the direction." "The company gave me the resources."

And yes, those things are true. You did not work in a vacuum. But the specific judgment calls, the specific interventions, the specific translations, the specific moments of "I saw the thing nobody else saw and I acted on it"—those were yours. The team was there. The resources were there. But the specific pattern recognition, the specific decision to act, the specific way you structured the solution—those came from your twenty years of accumulated tacit knowledge. No one else in the room made those same calls. You did.

Writing it down is not arrogance. It is accuracy. You are not claiming you did it alone. You are claiming you were the specific person whose intervention made the difference. That is a factual statement, not a boast.

The "Who Am I to Charge for This?" Question

There is a second psychological barrier specific to the packaging and pricing work. It goes like this: "This feels like something I should just do for people. It feels wrong to charge for advice that comes naturally to me. It feels like I'm monetizing a friendship, or monetizing common sense."

This belief has a specific origin, and it is worth tracing. For most of your career, your expertise was bundled into your salary. You gave your judgment, your pattern recognition, your translations, your shadow work, and in return you received a fixed biweekly paycheck. The exchange was invisible. You didn't feel like you were "selling" your advice because the transaction was mediated by the employment structure. You gave, the company paid, and the specific link between your Tuesday afternoon intervention and your Friday direct deposit was obscured.

Now you are being asked to make that link visible. To say, directly, to another human being: "My judgment on this specific problem is worth $X, and I will provide it in exchange for that amount." And that visibility feels uncomfortable because it strips away the organizational mediation that made the exchange feel impersonal and safe.

But the discomfort is not evidence that charging is wrong. The discomfort is evidence that you are doing something new—making a previously invisible transaction visible. And new things feel uncomfortable. That is not a signal to stop. It is a signal that you are outside your old pattern, which is exactly where growth happens.

The reframe is simple: you are not charging for "advice." You are charging for twenty years of compressed pattern recognition that allows you to see in four minutes what would take someone else four months to figure out through trial and error. You are not selling the four minutes. You are selling the twenty years that made the four minutes possible. And that is worth paying for.

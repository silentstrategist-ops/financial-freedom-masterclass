The Packaging Shift — Turning Raw Experience Into a Named, Priced Offer
The Difference Between a Skill and an Offer

A skill is something you can do. An offer is something someone can buy.

"I'm good at project management" is a skill. It is vague, undifferentiated, and impossible to price. Every project manager on LinkedIn says they are "good at project management." It tells a potential client nothing specific about what will change in their life or business if they hire you.

"I help Series B SaaS companies restructure their engineering delivery process to hit 90%+ sprint completion within two quarters, without adding headcount" is an offer. It is specific. It names the buyer (Series B SaaS companies). It names the problem (engineering delivery dysfunction). It names the outcome (90%+ sprint completion). It names the timeline (two quarters). It names the constraint (without adding headcount). A potential client reading that sentence knows exactly whether they have this problem, and whether you are the person who solves it.

The packaging shift is the act of translating your skills into offers. It is the act of moving from "here's what I can do" to "here's what changes for you if you work with me."

The Three Components of a Properly Packaged Offer

Every sellable offer, whether it is a consulting engagement, a coaching package, a freelance project, or a fractional role, contains three components:

1. The Specific Problem (The "Before" State).

What is the painful, expensive, urgent situation your client is currently in? Not a vague category of problem. A specific, felt, costly situation. "Your engineering team is missing deadlines and your CTO is threatening to quit" is a specific problem. "You have operational challenges" is not.

The more specific and painful the problem, the more the client will pay to solve it. People do not pay premium rates for "improvement." They pay premium rates for relief from a specific, expensive pain.

1. The Specific Outcome (The "After" State).

What will be measurably, observably different after working with you? Not "things will be better." A specific, tangible, ideally quantifiable change. "Sprint completion hits 90% within two quarters." "Your vendor costs drop by 18% within six months." "Your newly promoted VPs have a working delegation framework within 90 days and their first-year retention rate doubles."

The outcome is what the client is actually buying. They are not buying your time. They are not buying your expertise in the abstract. They are buying the delta between their current painful state and the future improved state. Your job in packaging is to make that delta as concrete and vivid as possible.

1. The Specific Mechanism (The "How").

What is your method for getting them from Before to After? This is where your tacit knowledge becomes visible. This is where your twenty years of pattern recognition, your specific frameworks, your particular way of diagnosing and intervening, become the differentiator. "I use a structured change-request protocol combined with weekly facilitated alignment sessions between product and engineering leads" is a mechanism. "I'll figure it out as we go" is not.

Your mechanism does not need to be proprietary or trademarked. It just needs to be yours—the specific, repeatable approach you have developed through experience that produces the outcome reliably. This is what separates you from a generic freelancer who says "I can help with that." You have a method. The method is the product.

The Naming Principle

A properly packaged offer has a name. Not a cute marketing name. A descriptive name that tells the buyer exactly what they are getting.

Weak: "Operations Consulting."

Strong: "The 90-Day Delivery Reset."

Weak: "Executive Coaching."

Strong: "The First-Year VP Transition Program."

Weak: "Financial Analysis for Small Business."

Strong: "The Margin Leak Audit: Finding Your Hidden 10%."

The name does three things simultaneously. It signals specificity (this is not generic). It signals a contained engagement (this has a beginning, a middle, and an end—it is not an open-ended retainer). And it makes the offer talkable—a client can say to a colleague, "I hired someone to do a Margin Leak Audit on our project profitability," and the colleague immediately understands what was done and whether they need it too. That talkability is the seed of referral. We will return to this in Chapter 11.

Pricing the Offer: The Floor-Price Principle

You are not ready to set your final market price yet. That comes in Chapter 9 (for consulting) and Chapter 10 (for coaching). But you need to establish your floor—the absolute minimum you will accept for your time and judgment, below which the engagement is not worth doing.

The floor-price formula is simple:

Floor Rate = (Your Current Annual Compensation + Benefits + Overhead) ÷ Billable Hours × 2.5

Why 2.5? Because as an independent consultant, coach, or freelancer, you are no longer just selling your labor. You are also covering your own benefits (health insurance, retirement contributions), your own overhead (software, workspace, professional development), your own non-billable time (marketing, admin, proposal writing), your own taxes (self-employment tax adds roughly 15%), and your own risk (no paid leave, no severance, no guaranteed next paycheck). The 2.5 multiplier is a conservative baseline. Many experienced consultants use 3x or higher.

Example: If your current total compensation (salary + benefits + bonus) is $160,000, and you assume 1,200 billable hours per year (roughly 25 hours per week of actual client-facing work, accounting for vacations, admin, and business development):

$160,000 ÷ 1,200 = $133/hour base.

$133 × 2.5 = $333/hour floor.

This is not your market rate. Your market rate may be significantly higher, depending on the specificity and urgency of the problem you solve. But this is the number below which you do not go. Ever. If a potential engagement works out to less than your floor rate on an hourly basis, you walk away. Not because you are arrogant. Because below that number, you are financially better off staying in your W-2 job, and the engagement will not generate the evidence your subconscious needs to believe this path is viable.

Write your floor number down. Keep it visible. It is the first boundary of your new professional identity.
